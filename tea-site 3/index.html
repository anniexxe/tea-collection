01 / INTRO

Tea as a time
of pause
and place.

Tea often enters everyday life through something very small: a paper package, a thin string, a printed tag, and a bag filled with dried leaves or flowers. These objects are familiar enough to become almost invisible. We open them, place them in water, wait for a few minutes, and often throw them away without looking closely. Yet behind this ordinary object is a much larger story.

A CUP OF A LARGER STORY AN ARCHIVE OF EVERYDAY

A Cup of a Larger Story begins with the tea bag as an everyday graphic object and asks what can be discovered by paying attention to it. Instead of looking at tea only as a drink, this collection considers the relationships between place, plant, process, design, time, and ritual. Each small booklet begins with one tea bag and follows a different part of the world contained within it.

Steuarts Vintage Earl Grey opens a story about place and history. The words “1835” and “Ceylon” connect a contemporary tea bag to Sri Lanka and to a company history extending far beyond the life of the package itself. Twinings Pure Camomile shifts attention toward the flower and toward time. Its story is not only about what camomile tastes like, but about the few quiet minutes required for dried botanical material to become an infusion.

TAZO Passion introduces another way of reading tea. Hibiscus, orange peel, rose hips, typography, and intense color create a sensory identity that begins before the drink is tasted. Roasted Oolong focuses on process. Heat, oxidation, movement, time, and water transform a leaf again and again before it becomes the final cup.

Together, these examples show that a tea bag is never only a tea bag. It is the final visible object in a much larger network. Plants have been grown and harvested. Materials have been processed. Ingredients have moved between places. Designers have selected colors, images, words, and typefaces. Packages have been printed, transported, opened, and eventually discarded.

01 — THE OBJECT · CEYLON / TAG / ORIGIN

Before the tea is brewed, the tag is already telling a story. The date 1835, the word Ceylon, the ornamental crest, and the serif typography create an immediate sense of history. These elements transform a small disposable label into a marker of identity.

The tag does not explain the entire history of the tea; instead, it compresses ideas of place, heritage, and tradition into an object only a few centimeters wide.

A PLACE OF ORIGINS

Ceylon, now Sri Lanka, has long been associated with tea-growing landscapes shaped by mountains, mist, and climate. The name continues to connect the tea in the cup to a specific place, carrying traces of geography, cultivation, and history into an everyday object.

02 — 1835 · HISTORY / PEOPLE / LAND

George Steuart & Company was established in Ceylon in 1835. Originally involved in the export of commodities including coffee, the company became closely connected to the island’s growing tea industry as coffee plantations declined in the 1870s.

Over time, tea became one of the defining parts of the Steuart legacy. Today, the name 1835 Steuarts Ceylon carries this history forward through its Ceylon tea blends, including Vintage Earl Grey.

A PLACE IN A NAME

Ceylon was the former name of Sri Lanka, but the word continues to appear in the language of tea. On this package, “Ceylon” works as both a place and an identity. It connects the finished product to the island where the tea originates while also carrying traces of an older history. The name therefore becomes part of the visual experience of the product, linking geography to branding in a single word.

PEOPLE AND LAND

The history of Ceylon tea is also a story of people. Generations of tea workers, planters, traders, and craftspeople shaped the industry and the landscapes that supported it. From cultivation and harvesting to processing and transport, many hands were involved before the tea reached its final form.

The cup we drink today carries traces of this long relationship between people, place, labor, and time.

03 — A CONTINUING STORY · BLEND / TASTE / TRADITION

What began in Ceylon in 1835 has grown into a lasting tea heritage. Through changing times, George Steuart & Company has remained connected to Sri Lanka and its long tradition of tea. Today, the Steuarts Ceylon name continues to bring this history into the present, connecting generations of tea culture with the simple ritual of making a cup.

The landscapes, people, and traditions behind Ceylon tea remain part of its identity. From the high-grown tea regions of Sri Lanka to the finished leaves inside each tea bag, every cup carries traces of its origin. Tea is not only a product of the plant itself, but also of place, climate, cultivation, processing, and time.

04 — MORE THAN A TEA · PLACE / TIME / EVERYDAY LIFE

A tea bag may seem like a small and ordinary object, but it carries a world within it — a place, a history, a craft, and the people who keep it alive. From the misty hills of Ceylon to your table today, each cup connects distant landscapes with everyday moments, reminding us that what we consume is never just one thing.

Tea travels across mountains, oceans, and generations. It changes form, but its origin remains. The leaves that once grew in the soils of Ceylon have journeyed far, finding new homes, new rituals, and new people. Yet in every cup, there is still a trace of that place — the climate, the land, and the hands that made it possible.

THE BLEND

Vintage Earl Grey combines Ceylon black tea with the distinctive fragrance of bergamot. The citrus character of bergamot adds brightness to the deeper flavor of the black tea, creating a blend that feels both familiar and refined.

THE TASTE

The tea has a fragrant citrus aroma, a smooth black-tea body, and a clean finish. The contrast between the richness of Ceylon tea and the freshness of bergamot gives Earl Grey its recognizable character.

A STORY THAT CONTINUES

From a company established in Ceylon in 1835 to a tea bag brewed today, the story continues through an everyday ritual. The package may be small, but inside it are layers of place, material, history, and tradition.

IN EVERYDAY MOMENTS

Tea is part of the quiet hours — a pause in the morning, a break in the afternoon, or a conversation in the evening. It adapts to our routines and changes with our lives, but it always offers the same simple gesture: a moment to slow down, to breathe, and to be present.

The story of Steuarts Ceylon does not end with this package. It continues in the fields, in the people, in the traditions, and in the cups that are shared around the world. As times change, the essence remains — quality, care, and a respect for the place it comes from. What began in 1835 is still unfolding, one cup at a time.

Scale

The word Ceylon occupies very little space on the Steuarts tag, yet it carries one of the largest ideas in this collection: place.

A tea bag can travel far from the landscape connected to the tea inside it. By the time it reaches a kitchen, café, hotel, or classroom, the physical distance between the drinker and the origin of the tea may be enormous. Packaging becomes one of the few remaining ways in which that distant place is made visible.

On the Steuarts tag, place is communicated through language rather than landscape. The word CEYLON appears directly beneath the company name. Above it sits the date 1835, surrounded by ornamental typography and a crest. Together these elements construct an identity based on history, location, and continuity.

The visual language is very different from a contemporary package that might emphasize freshness through bright color or simple geometric type. Steuarts instead uses graphic elements that appear historical. The date asks the viewer to look backward. The crest suggests institution and tradition. The serif lettering feels formal. The word Ceylon connects these signals to a geographical history.

This is important because the tea bag itself is temporary. It may exist in the drinker’s hands for only a few minutes. The graphic identity printed on its tag attempts to carry a history much longer than the life of the object.

Scale therefore becomes one of the central contrasts in this project. A landscape is large. A history can extend across centuries. Systems of cultivation and trade involve many people and places. Yet all of these ideas can be compressed into a label that fits between two fingers.

Vintage Earl Grey introduces another kind of movement. Ceylon black tea is combined with the recognizable citrus character of bergamot. The finished blend brings together origin, ingredient, brand, and an internationally familiar style of tea. The cup becomes a meeting point rather than the expression of only one location.

Camomile changes quietly from flower to infusion. Once dried, its petals and stems remain visible inside the tea bag. Hot water softens the flowers, releases their gentle aroma, and slowly turns the water golden. A small flower becomes a warm, calming cup.

Through the translucent material of the tea bag, the camomile remains visible as a plant. Small stems, flower centers, petals, and dried fragments can still be recognized. The bag contains the ingredient without completely hiding it, allowing the material inside to become part of the visual experience.

A FEW MINUTES

Brewing tea creates a small interruption in the rhythm of the day. After hot water is poured, there is very little left to do except wait. The flowers need time to soften, release their aroma, and slowly change the water around them. These few minutes may seem insignificant, but they create a space between one activity and the next.

WAITING IS PART OF THE CUP

The pause is not separate from the experience of tea. It is part of how the drink is made. Camomile cannot become an infusion instantly. Water, plant, and time have to remain together. During this quiet process, the cup gradually changes while the person preparing it simply waits.

THREE MINUTES

Three minutes can be measured easily, but they can also be experienced differently. In a busy day, they may disappear without notice. While tea is brewing, however, those minutes become visible. The cup gives waiting a beginning and an end.

WHAT REMAINS

The color of Passion is immediate. It appears in the package, in the dried hibiscus, and finally in the water. Yet the experience of tea continues beyond what can be seen. Aroma rises from the cup, temperature changes, ice slowly melts, and flavor develops as the drink is consumed. What begins as a visual impression gradually becomes a physical and sensory experience.

A tea bag is designed to disappear. It is opened, placed in water, removed, and eventually discarded. Its life as an object is brief. But during that short period, it connects several different systems: plants that have been grown and dried, ingredients that have been selected and combined, packaging that has been designed and printed, and a person preparing a drink.

FROM OBJECT TO EXPERIENCE

This movement from image to material is part of what makes the tea bag interesting as an object. Before brewing, design creates an expectation. Color suggests intensity, botanical imagery suggests ingredients, and typography gives the blend a particular personality. After brewing, those visual ideas are tested against the actual drink.

TAZO Passion makes these connections especially visible because its identity moves so clearly between graphic design and physical material. The reds and pinks of the package are echoed by hibiscus. The promise of brightness becomes visible as clear water changes color. The botanical imagery printed on the outside returns in another form through the fragments contained inside the bag.

The object therefore becomes more than packaging. It acts as a small introduction to everything that follows.

ACTION BECOMES FLAVOR

Oolong is not defined by one single flavor or appearance. It belongs to a broad family of teas shaped by the way the leaves are handled after harvesting. Different decisions made during processing can create very different results.

The transformation begins with moisture. Freshly picked leaves are allowed to wither, gradually becoming softer and less rigid. Movement and handling then change the physical structure of the leaf. As the material reacts with air, its color, aroma, and character begin to develop.

Rolling changes the leaf again, creating the twisted or compressed forms that remain visible after drying. Heat is eventually introduced to control the transformation and stabilize the material.

Every stage leaves a trace. Time affects aroma. Movement affects structure. Heat affects color and depth.

What the drinker later experiences as flavor is connected to a sequence of physical actions that happened long before water entered the cup.

BETWEEN HEAT AND TIME

Roasting does not transform the leaf all at once. Heat works gradually, allowing the material to change over time. As moisture continues to leave the leaf, its surface darkens and its aroma becomes deeper. The process is slow enough that small differences in time and temperature can shape the final character of the tea.

THE MATERIAL REMEMBERS

Even after the heat is gone, the leaf continues to carry evidence of the roast. Its darker color, dry surface, compact form, and warm aroma become physical traces of an earlier process. What remains in the finished tea is not the heat itself, but the changes it created.

THE ROAST

Roasting introduces another relationship between tea and time. Heat acts on material that has already been transformed through earlier stages of processing. The leaf becomes darker, its aroma changes, and the character of the finished tea develops further.

Roasting is important because it describes an action rather than an ingredient. Unlike words such as orange, mint, or camomile, the word “roasted” tells us that something has happened to the leaf. It points backward toward a process that is no longer visible when the package reaches the drinker. The finished leaf becomes evidence of that invisible action.

The
Cup

A CONTINUING TRANSFORMATION

A cup of roasted oolong contains several different forms of time: the time required for the plant to grow, the time of harvesting and processing, the time of roasting, and finally the short period of brewing.

These stages collapse into one everyday action. The drinker does not witness every earlier process, but the result of those processes remains present in the cup.

Tea is therefore not a static object. It changes before packaging, during brewing, and even while it is being consumed. Temperature falls, aroma shifts, leaves continue to open, and flavor develops from one sip to the next.

The cup is not the beginning of the story. It is one of its final transformations.

The movement between compression and expansion is also reflected in the physical structure of this publication.

Each small booklet is close to the scale of a tea package. When closed, it contains only a limited amount of visible information. The accordion behaves in the same way. Folded together, it is compact. Opened, it occupies a much larger space.

ABOUT THE BOOKCONCEPT & IDEA

This book explores tea culture through the small details of tea bags, tags, and packaging. I was interested in how such a simple everyday object can carry information about a brand, its history, origin, flavor, and visual identity.

Instead of making the book feel like a traditional tea guide, I designed it as a visual collection and archive. Each section focuses on a different tea or brand, using photographs, packaging details, typography, and short texts to show their individual character.

The small accordion-book format also connects to the idea of collecting and unfolding information. As the reader opens the book, the story gradually expands, turning a small tea bag into a larger story about design, culture, and everyday experience.
